What did we check?

Every concrete claim that AI 2027 dates before 2027 — in the story, its footnotes, boxes and panel of key figures, and its compute supplement — which came to 50 claims. A sentence that predicts several separable things (a chip count and a power figure, say) is split, and 10 claims became 23 parts. That makes 63 parts, one left out because its own date is December 2027.

The text graded is the one published on April 3, 2025, read from the Wayback Machine. Where the authors have since edited it, the original stands, and their later views are never used as evidence. The scenario’s 2027 is not graded, and a good record on 2025 and 2026 says little about how 2027 will go.

The scenario’s labs are fictional, so we choose a real one to stand in for each, by one test: the leading US lab is whichever of OpenAI, Anthropic and Google DeepMind has the best public model on Epoch AI’s capability index at the end of the period; if another’s score range overlaps, both are checked, and a disagreement makes the claim Can’t be judged. In China the candidates are DeepSeek, Alibaba, Moonshot AI, Zhipu AI and ByteDance. Labs outside these lists, such as xAI, never change a verdict.

What counts as “happened”?

A number has to land near the scenario’s. For “about X” or a bare figure, within 25% is a full match, and anything from half to double is Partly. Why so wide? Sources for the same AI figure often differ by 10–25%, and for quantities that grow 2 to 4 times a year, a factor of 2 is roughly a 6 to 12 month error. Benchmark scores get 5 points for a full match and 15 for Partly. Vague words get fixed meanings (“hundreds” is 200 or more), and a word with no plain number, like “unreliable”, is graded only if a measure for it was fixed beforehand.

“Mid 2025” means May to August 2025, and 3 months either side still counts as on time (the grace period). Four to nine months late is Partly and anything later is Didn’t happen, so a prediction whose months have passed stays open that long.

Whose numbers do we trust?

Official statistics, filings, benchmark leaderboards, research datasets, and labs’ and governments’ own statements come first, news outlets with editorial standards second, and social posts only as context. An undisputed fact needs one source of either kind, a disputed one an official source or two independent reports, and when a measurement and a summary of it disagree, the measurement wins.

Some of these choices lean toward the scenario, and three matter most. We accept a lab’s own benchmark score, even with no stated setup or on part of the test, unless an independent result for the same model differs by more than 5 points. We count an event that came early as a hit, since it did happen. And where a phrase was given two measures before grading, we use the one that favors the scenario. Five verdicts rest on a lab’s statement about its own model, two came months before their window, and eight lean on the grace period. Sixteen of the 21 hits and partial hits rest on a single source.

What can’t we judge, and why?

Eighteen parts are Can’t be judged. Two are secrets: how much AI speeds up research inside a lab, and how well a lab guards its model files. Three use a word with no agreed number (“unreliable”, “widespread”, “often”) and had no measure fixed in advance. The other thirteen are measurable, but the deciding figure has not turned up in a reliable source (nine), or the sources span too wide a range to settle it (four). Each row says what is missing.

Showing that something did not happen (“no more extreme incidents”) takes a search of the whole period and every lab. Ours was not complete.

How did we make this, and what would we do differently?

This is not pre-registration. We wrote a first rulebook and a list of claims, gathered evidence, revised the rulebook and ran a first grading pass. Then came a second revision, a fresh measure for each claim, a second pass, more evidence and an independent review, and the rules now in force were set after that review, with their effect on each verdict already known.

Most of those revisions favored the scenario. Of 21 documented changes, 13 have a known direction, and 11 of the 13 made a hit easier. One set of four, made after the review, moved 8 verdicts, all toward the scenario, and the last pass, on November 15, 2026, moved 3, all away from it. The early drafts held a few Didn’t happen verdicts, and a later change removed each one. The table as it stands can be downloaded from the Data page. The earlier rulebooks are kept, but not yet published.

What would we do differently? Fix every measure before collecting any evidence, and have a second person, blind to it, regrade the close calls. Extract the claims twice, independently, and read the other supplements as closely as the compute one. And weigh claims by size — as it stands, a side clause about Operator counts as much as a trillion-dollar valuation.

How do I dispute a verdict?

Name the prediction, the source we missed or misread, and the rule it affects. Send it to us on X: the About page has the link. We fix errors in place and date the fix.
